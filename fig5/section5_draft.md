# 5. Cooperative excursions beyond the harmonic cage

Draft. ⟦ ⟧ marks numbers or statements to check against the raw data.

Section 3 showed that each junction fluctuates about a fixed mean position in a Gaussian cage whose width σ_i differs from junction to junction. Pooling the displacements of many junctions therefore mixes cages of different widths, and the pooled distribution P(δx) has tails that no single Gaussian describes (Fig. 5b). To separate these tails from the static heterogeneity, we rescale each displacement by the width of its own junction, Δ = δx/σ_i, and pool about 10^6 rescaled displacements per condition. The rescaling is justified because σ_i is a property of the junction and not of the sampling. Widths measured from the first and second halves of the same trajectory agree (r = ⟦0.97⟧), while widths of different junctions vary by a factor of about three (Fig. 5c).

After rescaling, the core of the distribution P̂(Δ) falls on the unit Gaussian, with no adjustable parameter, in all twelve conditions we measured: three PEGDA precursor masses and a four-arm control, three swelling times and five temperatures (Fig. 5d–f). A Gaussian fit to |Δ| < 2 gives a width of 1.03 ± 0.02 (Fig. 5h). Up to about two cage widths, every junction is described by the phantom harmonic cage of Section 3 alone.

Beyond the core, P̂ decays far more slowly than the Gaussian. Displacements larger than 3σ_i are 3–10 times more frequent than a harmonic cage allows (E_3σ in Fig. 5h). Over the range we resolve, the tail is close to exponential, P̂ ∝ exp(−|Δ|/λ), with λ = 0.5–1.2. The departure begins at the same point in every condition. The ratio of P̂ to the unit Gaussian G stays at 1 up to |Δ| ≈ 2 and reaches 2 at Δ* = 2.63 ± 0.11 (mean ± s.d. over 12 conditions; Fig. 5g). In energy units, U = −k_BT ln P̂, the cage is harmonic up to U* = ½Δ*² ≈ 3.5 k_BT and then grows roughly linearly (Fig. 5a). Equivalently, the restoring force rises linearly inside the cage and saturates beyond Δ* at about 1.5 k_BT/σ_i. For the pooled cage widths this is 0.75, 0.54 and 0.46 pN for the 5, 10 and 20 kDa networks (Fig. 5a, inset; Supplementary Fig. ⟦X⟧).

The three control parameters act differently on the tail. Swelling for 0.5, 2 or 24 h leaves the whole distribution unchanged, both the onset and the tail (Fig. 5e). The core behaves the same way: its width σ does not depend on swelling (Section 3), as expected for phantom junctions, whose fluctuations do not depend on macroscopic deformation^⟦James & Guth⟧. Precursor mass and temperature change the weight of the tail but barely move its onset. E_3σ increases from 3.2 for 5 kDa to 7.6 and 5.9–10.3 for 10 and 20 kDa. It decreases on heating from 8.7 at 5 °C to 3.3 at 45 °C, while Δ* shifts only from 2.52 to 2.85 (Fig. 5f,h). The four-arm PEG-SH network has a lighter tail (E_3σ = 4.2) than PEGDA of comparable strand length ⟦10 kDa between junctions, assuming 5 kDa arms⟧. The same nominal 20 kDa sample measured on two independent gels gives E_3σ = 10.3 and 5.9. Differences smaller than about a factor of 1.7 between gels are therefore not yet significant ⟦replicates⟧.

No single-strand effect produces this shape. For Gaussian strands the phantom cage is harmonic at every displacement. Finite extensibility and good-solvent statistics make strands stiffer at large extension, which has the wrong sign, and excluded volume softens them uniformly, which has the wrong shape. The softening must come from what the strands are attached to. We propose that large excursions are cooperative. Most of the time the junction sits in its phantom cage (the closed state), and moving it means stretching its own f strands against fixed neighbours. Occasionally the neighbourhood rearranges as a whole: neighbouring junctions move in the same direction, and slack stored in nearby loops or dangling ends is released (Fig. 5a). While this lasts the cage is open. The strands between junctions that move together barely stretch, so the junction pays much less per unit displacement. The simplest form of this picture is a two-state cage,

P̂(Δ) = (1 − p) G(Δ; 1) + p G(Δ; s),    (1)

where G(Δ; s) is a normalised Gaussian of width s, p is the fraction of time (or of junctions) in the open state and s is the width of the open cage in units of σ_i. A distribution of open-cage widths would turn the Gaussian tail of the second term into an exponential one, which is the usual route to Laplace tails in heterogeneous diffusion^⟦Chubynsky & Slater 2014; Chechkin et al. 2017⟧. Within our dynamic range a single width suffices.

Equation (1) describes all twelve conditions (lines in Fig. 5d–f), with p = 0.05–0.14 and s = 1.7–2.7 for the PEGDA networks. The two parameters are correlated, but two combinations are well determined and have a direct meaning. First, the open cage is s² ≈ 3–7 times softer than the closed one. Written as springs in series, 1/k_open = 1/k_strands + 1/k_env, this means the rearranging neighbourhood is 2–7 times (typically 3–4 times) more compliant than the junction's own strands. Second, the cost of opening the cage is ε = ln s + ln[(1 − p)/p] = 3.0 ± 0.4 k_BT across the PEGDA conditions. The model predicts the crossover from these two quantities, Δ_c² = 2εs²/(s² − 1), giving Δ_c = 2.6–3.0 against the measured Δ* = 2.5–2.85. The crossover is thus the displacement at which stretching the junction's own strands further costs more than rearranging its neighbourhood. It is set by an energy of a few k_BT, which is why Δ* is nearly constant in units of σ_i.

Each invariance constrains what rearranges. Insensitivity to swelling, which dilutes the network ⟦about threefold⟧, rules out contacts or entanglements between interpenetrating strands. It points instead to connectivity: loops, dangling ends and neighbouring junctions. This is the phantom limit of the constrained-junction picture^⟦Flory 1977; Flory & Erman 1982⟧. Longer precursors give heavier tails, consistent with more stored slack per junction. The four-arm network's lighter tail is consistent with its lower density of loops and dangling ends^⟦Sakai et al. 2008; Zhong et al. 2016⟧ ⟦confirm chemistry⟧. Temperature is the most informative parameter. If large excursions were thermally activated escapes from the cage, as in supercooled liquids, they would become more frequent on heating. We observe the opposite. In the two-state fit ε shows no systematic trend with temperature (2.6–3.6 k_BT), and the open cage is narrowest at 45 °C (s = 1.7, against 2.1 at 5 °C), although s is not monotonic in between. We tentatively attribute this to the decrease in the solvent quality of water for PEG on heating, which leaves less slack available for rearrangement ⟦test: σ(T) and ℓ = λσ(T)⟧.

Most individual trajectories nonetheless look Gaussian, and this is expected. With ⟦60–180⟧ frames per junction, a trajectory samples only a few displacements beyond Δ*. Trajectories of this length drawn from the pooled P̂ are flagged as non-Gaussian by a Shapiro–Wilk test in only a quarter to a half of cases (median 25–50%; 10–85% across conditions), even though every one of them has the pooled tail. For Gaussian junctions the figure is 5% (Fig. 5i). A single short trajectory without a visible tail therefore does not identify a Gaussian subpopulation. Applied to our trajectories, the same test flags ⟦x⟧% of junctions, ⟦consistent with / below⟧ the homogeneous prediction. Two further tests separate junctions that switch between states from a subpopulation with permanently open cages. One is the clustering in time of large excursions within a trajectory; the other is whether the tail persists in the longest trajectories (Supplementary Fig. ⟦X⟧).

A Gaussian core with exponential tails is the signature of cage breaking near the glass and jamming transitions^⟦Chaudhuri, Berthier & Kob 2007; Weeks et al. 2000⟧ and of "Brownian yet non-Gaussian" diffusion^⟦Wang et al. 2012⟧. In those systems it is usually traced to a distribution of local mobilities. The analogy has limits here. Our displacements are bounded and stationary, and the tail weakens rather than grows with temperature. What this system adds is a harmonic baseline known with no free parameter, so the cooperative part can be isolated as an excess over the phantom cage and measured directly. In mechanical terms the open cage is a soft local mode of a disordered, nearly isostatic network^⟦Wyart 2005; Zaccone & Scossa-Romano 2011⟧. The series-compliance form above connects the two descriptions (Supplementary Note ⟦X⟧).

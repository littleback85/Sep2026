# Fig. 1 (revised): legend and notes

**Logic arc:** Coacervate dispersions are needed as liquids by the emerging uses but exist only at µL–mL scale, and the uses that reached scale do not need them to stay liquid (a). The dispersion is a waypoint because nothing holds it, and a CAP turns it into a place to stay, reached from either end of demixing (b). The architecture of the CAP then chooses which phase is continuous (c). Together these set up the rest of the paper: making the emulsions (Fig. 2), mapping them (Fig. 3), operating them (Figs 4–5) and using them (Fig. 6).

---

**Fig. 1 | Holding a coacervate dispersion as a liquid at litre scale.**
**a**, Where coacervate dispersions are used, placed by batch volume and by how long the dispersion stays a liquid before it coarsens, settles, deposits or is solidified. Filled envelopes are uses that need the dispersion held as a liquid; open envelopes are uses that do not, because the coacervate forms at the point of use (hair care), is crosslinked into a solid wall (microencapsulation), is applied as a bulk phase (wet adhesives) or is designed to settle (bioprocess extraction). Emerging uses (icons: extraction, microreactors, sensing, drug delivery, protocells) are carried out with bare coacervates that last minutes to hours, or with membranized coacervates that last days to weeks, and both are made at microlitre to millilitre scale. None of the surveyed uses lies in the litre-scale, days-held region (shaded; dashed lines at 1 L and 1 d). Open circle: CAP-stabilized coacervates from our previous work (ref.). Diamond and bars: CAP-held emulsions in this work (range of batch volume and storage time). Envelopes enclose the literature ranges collected in Supplementary Table X. *Aqueous two-phase systems separate by segregation and are not coacervates; they are shown because Fig. 6 compares all-aqueous separations.
**b**, Without a stabilizer, complex coacervation proceeds from solution through a transient droplet dispersion to two bulk layers within minutes to hours. A condensate-amphiphilic block copolymer (CAP) at the coacervate–water interface holds the dispersion. The held state can be reached in two ways. Adding the CAP to the feed solutions arrests droplet growth at nucleation and gives nanodroplets. Shearing bulk layers in the presence of the CAP gives micrometre to millimetre droplets (Fig. 2).
**c**, The CAP is a poly(ethylene glycol) corona joined to a random anchor of phenylboronic acid (B, filled) and amidoamine (M, open) units that sits in the coacervate. The ratio of corona to anchor sets which phase is continuous. Longer anchors favour water-in-coacervate (W/C) emulsions and larger PEG blocks favour coacervate-in-water (C/W), so the phase that hosts the larger block becomes continuous. The dotted line marks inversion. The measured state map is shown in Fig. 3. C, coacervate phase; W, dilute aqueous phase.
